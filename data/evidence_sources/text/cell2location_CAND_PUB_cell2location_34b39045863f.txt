T
he cellular architecture of tissues, where distinct cell types 
are organized in space, underlies cell–cell communication, 
organ function and pathology. Emerging spatial transcrip
-
tomics technologies provide key opportunities to map resident cell 
types and cell signaling in situ in a scalable manner. Despite existing 
proof-of-concept applications of these technologies
1–6, it remains 
a challenge to define spatial transcriptomics workflows that allow 
comprehensive mapping of resident cell types in tissues. One reason 
is the enormous cellular diversity across organs, including myriad 
fine-grained cell types such as immune
7,8, stromal 7,9 and neuro -
nal10,11 subpopulations. These fine cell types, marked by subtle tran-
scription differences, are often specialized across tissues and yield 
large potential for discoveries. Another reason is the diversity of 
spatial tissue architecture, ranging from the brain featuring discrete 
anatomical regions with distinct cell types to immune organs fea
-
turing dynamically modified microenvironments with fine-grained 
cell types. To create comprehensive cellular wiring diagrams of tis-
sues with spatial transcriptomics, it is necessary to establish experi-
mental and computational methods that are sufficiently sensitive to 
resolve fine-grained differences in cell types across diverse tissues.
Strategies that generate coupled single-cell and spatially resolved 
transcriptomics offer a scalable approach to address these chal
-
lenges. The key principle is to first identify resident cell types 
based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) from dissoci
-
ated tissues and then map the identified cell types to their tissue 
positions in situ based on spatial transcriptomic profiles
12. Here, 
spatial RNA-seq technologies, such as Visium 13, HDST14 and Slide 
sequencing (Slide-seq) 2, where mRNAs are positionally captured 
from thin tissue sections using microarray or bead array grids, pro-
vide transcriptome-wide data at high throughput and rely on simple 
histology and molecular biology protocols.
A key limitation of existing grid-based profiling methods is the 
lack of single-cell resolution, as spatial RNA-seq measurements 
combine multiple cells (Visium and Tomo-Seq)
13,15 or fractions 
of multiple cells (Slide-seq and HDST) 2,14 at each tissue location. 
This challenge can be overcome by the computational integration 
of spatial RNA-seq with the reference transcriptome signatures of 
cell types obtained from coupled scRNA-seq profiles. Although, 
recently, several computational methods have been proposed for 
this task
1–3,5,6, it is not clear whether these methods offer sufficient 
sensitivity to resolve fine-grained cell types in complex tissues, 
which is critical to make full use of the discovery potential of spatial 
transcriptomic data. Additionally, different confounding sources 
of variation need to be accounted for, which enables more com
-
plex integration tasks of data that consist of multiple experiments, 
including variable cell numbers across tissue positions and variable 
total mRNA content across cell types and individual cells
16,17.
To address the aforementioned challenges, we present cell2lo -
cation, a Bayesian model that is designed to resolve fine-grained 
cell types in spatial transcriptomic data and create cellular maps 
of diverse tissues. Cell2location can handle complex experimental 
settings, performing joint analysis of multiple scRNA-seq and spa
-
tial transcriptomic datasets. Unique to the model is a hierarchical 
design to borrow statistical strength across locations, which yields

Spatial transcriptomic technologies promise to resolve cellular wiring diagrams of tissues in health and disease, but compre-
hensive mapping of cell types in situ remains a challenge. Here we present сell2location, a Bayesian model that can resolve 
fine-grained cell types in spatial transcriptomic data and create comprehensive cellular maps of diverse tissues. Cell2location 
accounts for technical sources of variation and borrows statistical strength across locations, thereby enabling the integration 
of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics with higher sensitivity and resolution than existing tools. We assessed cell2location 
in three different tissues and show improved mapping of fine-grained cell types. In the mouse brain, we discovered fine regional 
astrocyte subtypes across the thalamus and hypothalamus. In the human lymph node, we spatially mapped a rare pre-germinal 
center B cell population. In the human gut, we resolved fine immune cell populations in lymphoid follicles. Collectively, our 
results present сell2location as a versatile analysis tool for mapping tissue architectures in a comprehensive manner.

Cell2location: a Bayesian model for spatial mapping of cell types. 
Cell2location maps the spatial distribution of cell types by integrat-
ing single-cell or nuclei RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic data 
from a given tissue (Fig. 1a). The first step of the cell2location work
-
flow is to estimate reference cell type signatures from single-cell and 
single-nucleus RNA-seq profiles, which correspond to gene expres
-
sion profiles for a set of user-provided cell types and subpopula -
tions identified using conventional clustering or other methods. By 
default, cell2location uses negative binomial regression for this step, 
which allows robust combination of data across technologies and 
batches (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 1). In a second step, cell
-
2location uses these reference signatures and one or multiple spa -
tial transcriptomic datasets as input to decompose mRNA counts 
at individual spatial locations into reference cell types (Fig. 1a and 
Supplementary Fig. 1).
Although cell2location is related to existing transcriptomic 
decomposition methods
1,3,5,6,18, it offers features to (1) borrow sta -
tistical strength across locations with similar cell composition, 
(2) account for batch variation across slides as well as variation 
in mRNA detection sensitivity and (3) estimate absolute cell type 
abundances by incorporating prior information about the analyzed 
tissues (see Supplementary Methods for details). In addition, cell
-
2location is computationally efficient, owing to variational approxi-
mate inference and GPU acceleration. The cell2location software is 
integrated with the scvi-tools framework
19 and comes with a suite of 
downstream analysis tools, including the identification of groups of 
co-located cell types.
To validate cell2location, we initially considered simulated data 
that were generated to reflect diverse cell abundance patterns across 
ubiquitous and spatially restricted cell types (Fig. 1b). In brief, 
we created a spatial transcriptomics dataset with 2,500 locations 
by sampling cells from 49 reference cell types, using a combina
-
tion of ubiquitous abundance patterns and cell types distributed 
according to smooth ‘tissue zones’ as observed in real data (Fig. 1b, 
Supplementary Fig. 2 and Methods). Reference cell type signatures 
and other simulation parameters were adapted from a mouse brain 
scRNA-seq dataset (Methods). Of note, unlike most benchmark 
datasets on real data (see below), this synthetic dataset provides 
ground truth information on the presence and absence of cell types 
as well as on quantitative abundance.
We then used the cell2location to estimate cell type proportions 
for different cell types at individual locations. Comparison of these 
estimates to the simulated (true) cell type proportions revealed that 
cell2location mapped cell types with high accuracy, irrespective of 
the cell abundance pattern (Fig. 1b), including low abundant and 
regional cell types.
Next, we compared cell2location to recently proposed meth
-
ods for the estimation of relative cell type abundance from spa -
tial transcriptomics data (Stereoscope 3, Seurat 1, RCTD 5, NNLS 
(AutoGeneS)18,20 and SpotLIGHT 6). Across the considered cell 
abundance patterns, we found that cell2location yielded more accu-
rate estimates of the cell type proportions across locations than other 
methods (Fig. 1c). In addition to this quantitative assessment of cell 
type proportions, we also assessed the ability of alternative methods 
to determine locations where individual cell types are present—that 
is, have non-zero cell abundance—which yielded a consistent rank
-
ing of methods (Supplementary Fig. 3b).
We note that, in addition to estimating relative cell type propor-
tions, cell2location can incorporate prior information about the 
tissue to estimate absolute cell type abundance, which, again, were 
highly concordant with the simulated ground truth (Supplementary 
Fig. 4a). Finally, we explored how specific design choices of cell
-
2location contribute to its performance (Supplementary Fig. 4b), 
and we assessed the robustness of the model to incomplete refer -
ence cell type sets (Supplementary Fig. 4c) as well as variation in 
hyper-parameter settings (Supplementary Fig. 5).
Cell2location accurately maps mouse brain cell types. To validate 
cell2location in a real tissue, we first applied the model to data from 
the mouse brain, which features diverse neural cell types organized 
in a well-characterized spatial architecture across brain areas, thus 
presenting a canonical use case to test spatial genomics
1,3,5,6. We gen-
erated matched single nucleus (sn) and Visium spatial RNA-seq (10x 
Genomics) profiles of adjacent mouse brain sections that contain 
multiple regions from the telencephalon and diencephalon (Fig. 2a). 
To assess the biological and intra-organ technical variation in spa
-
tial mapping, we assayed two mouse brains and serial tissue sections 
from each brain (total of three and two matched sections from two 
animals, respectively, and an extra section for snRNA-seq), creating 
a rich multi-modal and replicated transcriptomic dataset.

applied a conventional scRNA-seq workflow followed by Louvain 
clustering (Methods). This identified 51 putative cell type clusters, 
which were well represented across replicates (Supplementary Fig. 6).

brain (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Methods), including astro

Next, to assess the specificity of cell2location and alterna
-
tive methods, we mapped a large existing scRNA-seq dataset that 
includes cell types from multiple brain regions
11, only some of 
which are present in our spatial data (Fig. 2i). Consequently, this 
dataset spans ‘positive’ cell types from the somatosensory (SSp) cor
-
tex (n = 98) as well as ‘negative’ cell types that are exclusively present 
in the hippocampus (n = 23)11. When mapping these cell types to 
our SSp cortex Visium data, we would expect that negative cell types 
are excluded and only positive cell types are assigned substantial cell 
type proportions.
Indeed, cell2location mapping yielded substantially lower cell 
proportion estimates for the negative compared to the positive cell 
types (Fig. 2j; positive/negative fold change = 5.1). By contrast, 
other methods yielded a substantially weaker contrast between neg
-
ative and positive cell types (Fig. 2j; positive/negative fold change = 
0.7–1.9 for other methods; Supplementary Fig. 11). This indicates 
that cell2location accurately excludes reference cell types that should 
not be present in a spatial dataset, whereaas other methods tend 
to ‘overfit’ reference cell types. We also used cell type proportion 
estimates derived from the scRNA-seq data, which were generated 
from manually dissected cortical layers, to confirm the quantitative 
cell type proportions estimated by cell2location (Supplementary 
Fig. 11 and Supplementary File 7).
Finally, to show the versatility of cell2location across spatial tran
-
scriptomic technologies, we mapped our mouse brain snRNA-seq 
reference to a Slide-seq dataset of the mouse brain that offers 10-µm 
spatial resolution
25. Cell2location resolved cell types in Slide-seq 
spatial data, including fine anatomical subtypes across hippocampal 
divisions and cortical layers (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 12a and 
Supplementary Files 8 and 9). Moreover, cell2location revealed that 
more than 50% of Slide-seq ‘beads’ (that is, 10-µm-diameter spa
-
tial locations) contained more than one cell type (Supplementary 
Fig. 12b), consistent with previous observations5.
Collectively, our results show that cell2location maps diverse 
brain cell types with high accuracy and reproducibility and that the 
method is broadly applicable to spatial transcriptomic technologies.
Identification of regional astrocyte subtypes. Next, we assessed 
cell2location for mapping fine-grained cell types in the mouse 
brain. We focused on the heterogeneity of astrocytes, which is the 
largest class of glial cells that intimately regulate neuronal circuit 
development and function
26. Although astrocytes were historically 
considered to be a homogeneous cell type, recent evidence sug -
gests that astrocytes are regionally diversified across the brain 26, 
including fine anatomical divisions such as cortical layers 27–29. 
However, the extent of the regional heterogeneity of astrocytes is 
largely unknown, as existing studies have primarily compared their 
 
prevalence across selected brain areas30,31 or described broad astro -
cyte subtypes across major brain divisions (for example, telencepha-
lon versus diencephalon)10,32.
To identify regionally enriched astrocytes, we annotated and 
spatially mapped ten molecularly and spatially distinct astrocyte 
subtypes that were identified based on our snRNA-seq reference. 
In brief, we considered 3,013 cells annotated as astrocytes in our 
initial analysis (Fig. 2b). We then applied BBKNN
33 to further 
refine the integration of these cells across all six snRNA-seq data -
sets (Methods), followed by Leiden clustering, which identified 17 
transcriptomically defined subclusters (Supplementary Fig. 13). 
Next, we performed an initial mapping of these putative cell sub
-
types using cell2location, merging clusters that were not sufficiently 
distinct in both their mapped spatial locations and marker gene 
expression profiles (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14 and Methods). 
This resulted in ten molecularly and spatially distinct astrocyte sub
-
types (Fig. 3a,b and Supplementary Fig. 15), which we considered 
for further analysis. The extent of molecular heterogeneity across 
these subtypes (measured by the number of marker genes) was sub
-
stantially lower than across neuronal subpopulations, confirming 
the transcriptionally fine-grained nature of these cell populations 
(Supplementary Fig. 13d).
We identified gray matter astrocyte subtypes that were spatially 
enriched in the thalamus (THAL), hypothalamus (HYPO), cor
-
tex (CTX), amygdala (AMY), hippocampus (HPC) and striatum 
(STR) as well as white matter astrocytes (Fig. 3b and Supplementary 
 
Fig. 15). These subtypes expressed expected regional astrocyte 
markers
10, such as Foxg1 in the telencephalon (CTX, AMY , HPC 
and STR) and Slc6a11 and Agt in the diencephalon (THAL and 
HYPO) (Supplementary Fig. 15). Moreover, they stratified to finer 
regional subtypes based on the expression of subtype-specific genes 
such as Angpt1 and combinatorially expressed markers such as 
Gria1 (Supplementary Fig. 15; see below).
We focused on diencephalic astrocyte subtypes, as only one type 
of gray matter astrocyte subtype has been described in this brain 
area to date
10. Notably, our data revealed four regionally distinct 
gray matter astrocyte subpopulations across the diencephalon 
(Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 15). HYPO astrocytes were 
mainly located in the hypothalamus and, to a lesser extent, in 
the midline thalamic nuclei and the lateral habenula, and they 
were marked by Ttll3 expression. Three THAL subtypes occu
-
pied different regions of the thalamus. THAL_hab astrocytes, a 
rare cell population in our reference (Supplementary Fig. 6d), 
were restricted to the medial habenula and ventral white matter 
tracts and specifically expressed Angpt1 . THAL_med astrocytes 
were enriched in the medial thalamic nuclei and were marked by 
Lgr6 expression. Finally, THAL_lat astrocytes, although present 
throughout the thalamus and the subthalamus, were most preva
-
lent in ventrolateral thalamic nuclei and were marked by elevated 
Aldh1a1 expression compared to other subtypes (Fig. 3c and 
Supplementary Fig. 15).

Resolving the cellular compartments of the human lymph node. 
To test cell2location on diverse tissue architectures, we applied the 
model to spatially map tissue microenvironments of the human 
lymph node. Unlike the mouse brain, the human lymph node is 
characterized by dynamic microenvironments with many spa
-
tially interlaced cell populations. We analyzed a publicly available 
Visium dataset of the human lymph node from 10x Genomics34 and 
spatially mapped a comprehensive atlas of 34 reference cell types 
derived by integration of scRNA-seq datasets from human sec
-
ondary lymphoid organs composed of 73,260 cells 35–37 (Fig. 4a,b , 
Supplementary Fig. 19 and Methods).
Histological examination of the lymph node Visium sample 
revealed multiple germinal centers (GCs). Correspondingly, after 
mapping with cell2location, the expected major regions within the 
lymph node tissue could be readily identified, including T cell and 
B cell zones and GCs with follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) (Fig. 4c, 
right panel, and Supplementary Files 10 and 11).
Next, we used the cell2location mapping results to identify the 
spatial co-occurrence of cell types to better understand the tis
-
sue organization and predict cellular interactions. We performed 
non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) of the cell type abundance 
estimates from cell2location (Fig. 4d). Similarly to the established 
benefits of applying NMF to conventional scRNA-seq
38,39, the addi-
tive NMF decomposition yielded a grouping of spatial cell type 
abundance profiles into components that capture co-localized cell 
types. This NMF-based decomposition naturally accounts for the 
fact that multiple cell types and microenvironments can co-exist at 
the same Visium locations (Supplementary Fig. 20) while sharing 
information across tissue areas (for example, individual GCs).
We observed that the groups of cell types identified by NMF cor
-
responded to known functionally relevant cellular compartments of 
the lymph node (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, the NMF output enabled 
the dissection of the spatial compartments of B cell maturation. 
Specifically, naive B cells were mapped to a B follicle zone distinct 
from GCs (Fig. 4e , panel 1). We also identified the GC dark zone 
where B cells clonally expand and proliferate (Fig. 4e , panel 2) as 
well as the GC light zone where they undergo selection by T follicu
-
lar helper cells and FDCs to differentiate into antibody-producing 
plasmablasts (Fig. 4e, panel 3).
In addition to confirming known biology, the cell2location 
combined with the NMF decomposition enabled the fine-grained 
spatial mapping of a rare secondary activation B cell state, which 
was recently reported as primed to undergo class switch recom
-
bination preceding the GC entry 37,40. Our data indicate that this 
population (termed here ‘preGC’) occupies a different tissue 
compartment from the upstream activated cells and downstream 
GC cells (Fig. 4e, panel 4), suggesting that it is more likely to initiate 
new GC reactions rather than to join existing ones. Finally, our analy-
sis was capable of distinguishing blood vessel zones containing endo-
thelial and vascular smooth muscle cells (Supplementary Fig. 21a, 
panel 5) from the adjacent perivascular zone enriched in T helper 
and B memory cells (Supplementary Fig. 21a, panel 6).
Taken together, our results show that cell2location can map 
complex tissues characterized by spatially interlaced cell types and 
aid biological interpretation by identifying putatively interacting 
 
cell types.
Benchmarking cell2location for mapping fine immune cell 
types. Cell atlasing studies have greatly expanded the catalog of 
fine-grained cell types across human tissues, which are often of 
key interest for understanding tissue function and pathology
7,9,11. 
To quantitatively benchmark cell2location and alternative methods 
for mapping transcriptionally fine-grained cell types, we considered 
immune cell populations across two human tissues where many 
 
fine subtypes with specialized immune functions can be identi -
fied7–9. For example, T follicular helper cells differ from the clas -
sical CD4 + T cells in their ability to enter the GCs and guide B 
cell differentiation into plasma cells and memory B cells to ensure 
long-lasting immunity41.
Initially, we examined GC-specific immune cell types in the 
human lymph node (Fig. 4). We classified six known GC cell types 
as either transcriptionally fine or transcriptionally distinct, based 
on the Euclidean distance of their expression signatures to the clos-
est other cell type (Supplementary Fig. 22a and Methods). We anno-
tated the anatomical locations of GCs using Visium histology images 
(Fig. 4f) and assessed the accuracy of alternative methods in map
-
ping the six GC cell types to the expected GC locations. Although all 
considered methods achieved high accuracy for mapping distinct 
cell types such as FDCs (Fig. 4h), cell2location was markedly more 
accurate in mapping fine-grained cell types such as GC T follicular 
helper cells (Fig. 4f–h and Supplementary Figs. 22–24).
To extend our benchmarking to a second tissue with a 
larger number of fine-grained cell types, we examined immune 
cell populations in the human gut (Fig. 5a and Supplementary 
 
Figs. 25 and 26). The human gut presents a challenging applica -
tion for spatial mapping as it contains a larger number of tran -
scriptionally fine-grained immune and non-immune cell types, 
with the immune cells enriched in relatively small tissue areas 
across lymphoid follicles (for example, ~1% of tissue locations in 
our dataset).

Discussion
The integration of disassociated single-cell and spatial transcrip -
tomics promises a high-throughput approach to map tissue cell 
types and cell signaling in situ. To best use the potential of these 
technologies and to create comprehensive cellular wiring diagrams 
of tissues, computational models for high-resolution mapping of cell 
types in spatial transcriptomic data are needed. Here we presented 
cell2location, a Bayesian model that provides sufficient sensitivity 
and resolution to spatially map a wide range of cell types, includ
-
ing transcriptionally fine subtypes, thereby allowing the creation of 
comprehensive atlases of diverse tissues in a versatile manner.
We performed extensive quantitative benchmarks across three 
tissues, consistently showing that cell2location enables a substantial 
advance for spatially mapping fine-grained cell types over existing 
tools. In the human lymph node and gut, cell2location provided 
higher accuracy to resolve fine immune cell populations. In the 
mouse brain, cell2location enabled accurate mapping of fine astro
-
cyte subtypes.
Despite showing a small number of transcriptional differences, 
fine cell types often exhibit specialized and critical functions across 
tissues. In the immune system, we showed that cell2location can 
accurately resolve fine subtypes such as Tfh cells guiding B cell dif
-
ferentiation and FCRL4+ B cells modulating immune responses at 
mucosal sites44. In the mouse brain, we resolved four regional astro-
cyte populations. In addition to their molecular and spatial identi -
ties, we can only speculate about the regionalized functions of these 
astrocytes. Astrocyte–neuron signaling in the lateral habenula regu
-
lates neuronal circuits implicated in depression46. Similarly, dience-
phalic astrocytes could shape the activity of local neuronal circuits, 
such as the lateral thalamic circuitry for sensory relay or medial tha
-
lamic circuits involved in attention control47.
Although cell2locaiton is conceptually related to existing meth -
ods, specific design choices enable the benefits highlighted by 
our benchmarking results. Among the most important features, 
cell2location allows the borrowing of statistical strength across 
 
locations with similar cell composition, thereby improving sensitiv-
ity for mapping fine cell types. Additionally, the model can jointly 
process data across batches while accounting for technical differ
-
ences as well as variation in mRNA detection sensitivity. Finally, we 
note that cell2location is implemented as robust, fast and versatile 
software, integrated with scvi-tools framework
19, enabling a wide 
range of different applications.
Although we showed that cell2location can deal with data from 
a range of existing technologies, upcoming technologies will moti -
vate further refinements of the model. This includes support for 
higher-resolution spatial transcriptomics assays, for example by 
adapting the model to account for differences in the noise charac
-
teristics such as background probe binding or variable capture areas 
(for example, Nanostring
48,49 and LCM-RNA-seq 50). The modular 
implementation of cell2location in pyro and pymc3 (refs. 51,52) will 
facilitate such efforts.
A major future frontier for cell atlas projects is the 
three-dimensional (3D) mapping of tissues at cellular resolution. 
The spatial mapping approach described here can lay the foun
-
dations for 3D tissue atlases. Single-cell and spatial RNA-seq and 
imaging can be applied to consecutive tissue sections across an 
organ, integrating data from all technologies to build a multi-scale 
atlas. Regional cell type maps generated with cell2location can also 
guide gene selection for intact tissue volume imaging
53, leading to 
3D models of cells in their native tissue context with whole tran -
scriptome information.
In summary, we introduced here a versatile and flexible approach 
for spatial mapping of tissues. We expect that cell2location will have 
diverse applications in mapping tissue architecture across develop-
ment, health and disease. In separate applications to the human 
endometrium54, developing human gut 42 and human gonads 55, we 
further showed the versatility of cell2location to map fine-grained 
cell type differences across diverse tissues. Going forward, spatial 
cell type maps coupled with cell interaction analysis can help deci
-
pher developmental processes such as cellular differentiation and 
how it is regulated by extrinsic cues. These developmental cues can 
be recapitulated in vitro to refine tissue engineering approaches. 
Similarly, understanding how tissue architecture is perturbed in 
response to disease can enrich understanding of pathological pro
-
cesses and aid the design of therapeutic interventions.

signatures. The model takes an untransformed spatial expression count matrix

Note on the construction of reference cell type signatures. It is important to aim for a 
comprehensive and detailed cell-type reference, which includes as many of the cell 
types and subpopulations that are present in situ as possible. This can be achieved 
by generating a paired snRNA-seq reference from the same tissue sample. However, 
imperfect matching of cell populations is often acceptable (Figs. 4 and 5 and 
Supplementary Fig. 4c).
Based on one or multiple snRNA-seq input datasets, reference cell type 
signatures (gene expression) are estimated based on a defined set of cell 
populations, which, in turn, can be identified using conventional clustering 
workflows. By default, the cell2location employs a negative binomial regression to 
estimate reference cell type signatures. This model-based approach allows robust 
combinination of data across technologies and batches (Supplementary Fig. 19), 
which results in improved spatial mapping accuracy (Supplementary Figs. 24a–c 
and 25e). Alternatively, if batch effects are not a concern, a hard-coded method that 
estimates per-cluster average mRNA counts without any model-based strategy can 
be sufficient. This strategy is simple and fast and yields similar levels of accuracy if 
batch effects are small (Supplementary Fig. 24a–c). The hard-coded method is also 
recommended for non-unique molecular identifier (UMI) technologies for which 
the negative binomial count model is often not appropriate, such as Smart-seq2.
Note on determining model hyper-parameters. The cell2locaiton model has two 
parameters that should be adjusted by the user, taking known experimental and 
biological characteristics of a given dataset into consideration:
 1. Expected cell abundance per location 
ˆN
 2. Regularization of within-experiment variation in RNA detection sensitivity αy
Supplementary Fig. 27 provides a flowchart to guide the user to determine 
appropriate settings for these hyper-priors, which were used to determine the 
model parameters for all presented results.
The expected cell abundance ˆN per location is provided as a single tissue-level 
estimate, which can be derived from histology images (H&E or DAPI), ideally 
paired to the spatial expression data or at least representing the same tissue type. 
This parameter can be estimated by manually counting nuclei in 10–20 locations 
in the histology image (for example, using the 10x Loupe browser; Supplementary 
Fig. 8) and computing the average cell abundance. An appropriate setting of this 
prior is essential to inform the estimation of absolute cell type abundance values; 
however, the model is robust to a range of similar values (Supplementary Fig. 5). 
In settings where suitable histology images are not available, the size of capture 
regions relative to the expected size of cells can be used to estimate 
ˆN (Slide-seq 
V2; Supplementary Fig. 27). For all analyses in this manuscript, a single tissue-level 
estimate was used; however, as an advanced feature, cell2location can use the 
per-location number of cells.
Hyper-parameter α
y for regularizing within-experiment variation in RNA 
detection sensitivity. αy is the shape parameter of the prior gamma distribution 
over ys that represents the extent to which RNA detection sensitivity for individual 
locations deviates from the mean sensitivity of each batch. Large values of αy 
correspond to small within-experiment variability (Supplementary Methods). 
By default, we assume small within-experiment variability in RNA detection 
sensitivity across locations (αy = 200), which results in values of ys close to the 
mean sensitivity for each experiment. However, when strong gradients in mRNA 
detection sensitivity are observed, which is a common issue in adult human 10x 
Visium data, we recommend less strict regularization (α
y = 20).
Although good choices of these hyper-parameters can have a positive effect 
on accuracy and sensitivity, the estimates are robust to a range of suboptimal 
choices (Supplementary Fig. 5). The estimation of absolute cell abundance requires 
appropriate settings of 
ˆN in particular.
Validation of cell2location using synthetic data and comparison to alternative 
methods. Constructing a synthetic spatial transcriptomics dataset. Simulated spatial 
transcriptomics data were generated by combining expression profiles of cells 
drawn from each of 49 cell types in the mouse brain snRNA-seq reference data (see 
below), generating abundance profiles at 2,500 locations. snRNA data from the two 
most mouse brain snRNA-seq samples that had minimal batch effect were split into 
one dataset used to generate the synthetic data (50% of cells) and a second dataset 
used to evaluate cell2location and alternative methods (50% of cells), similarly to 
the strategy proposed by Andersson et al.
3. Hyper-parameters for data simulation 
were chosen to mimic (the typically low) cell counts observed for cell types in real 
tissues, additionally matching empirically observed sparsity profiles. Cell type 
abundances were simulated according to either a spatially ubiquitous pattern (eight 
cell types) or a regional pattern (41 cell types). Regional patterns are represented 
by 12 tissue zones defined by co-located cell types that mimic the organization 
of real tissues. The assignment of 41 regional cell types to the 12 tissue zones is 
shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, with each cell type belonging to 1–3 tissue zones 
and each tissue zone containing 2–8 cell types. The number of cell types present at 
each location, as well as the absolute abundance (the number of cells per location), 
were simulated according to either low or high average cell type abundance 
(Fig. 1b), stratified by ubiquitous and regional location patterns (see below). The 
mathematical description and the step-by-step procedure for simulating abundance 
of cell types across locations and generating multi-cell mRNA counts is described 
in detail in the Supplementary Methods (section 5).
Validation of cell2location using synthetic data. Synthetic gene expression data were 
constructed as described above to assess the performance of cell2location and 
alternative methods. The synthetic data provide ground truth values for the relative 
cell abundance of each cell type (cell type proportions), absolute cell abundance 
(including partial cells) and total mRNA count of each cell type for each location, 
which were used to benchmark estimates obtained from cell2location and 
alternative methods. All considered models were provided with the ground truth 
snRNA-seq reference data (evaluation subset, see above; Table 1) as well as the 
synthetic mRNA count matrices for all locations.
Cell2location. Signatures of reference cell types were derived using a negative 
binomial regression (Supplementary Methods) with default parameters, except 
training mini-batch size of 2,500 and training duration of 600 epochs. The 
hyper-parameter values used for the synthetic data analysis are shown in Table 1. 
In addition, the sensitivity of cell abundance estimates to the choices of these 
hyper-parameters was assessed (Supplementary Fig. 5, section on sensitivity and 
robustness below). The prior cell abundance 
ˆN = 9 was set to a value close to, but 
not fully matching, the ground truth (ˆN = 12), with the aim to obtain as realistic

Stereoscope3. This method uses a model-based approach to estimating both 
the cell type signatures and the relative cell abundance across locations (see the 
comparison in the Supplementary Methods). A negative binomial observation 
model is parameterized using a gene- and cell-type-specific log-odds parameter and 
a gene-specific total count parameter. This custom signature estimation method is 
hard-coded into the workflow, and both estimated parameters are used as input to 
the model for estimating relative cell abundance. Unless stated otherwise, default 
values of hyper-parameters are used. The scvi-tools
19 implementation of the 
Stereoscope model was used with the following hyper-parameters (default scvi-tools 
values were modified to achieve improved accuracy):
• No highly variable gene (HVG) selection was used (12,054 genes shared 
between single-cell and spatial data)
• Estimation of expression signatures of cell types: 0.01 learning rate; 1,500 epochs 
(increased compared to default to observe convergence); 1,024 mini-batch 
training size (similar to original Stereoscope default values).
• Estimation of relative cell abundance: 0.01 learning rate; 30,000 epochs (also 
increased compared to default); 1,250 mini-batch training size (half of the total 
number of locations, similar to original Stereoscope default values).

SPOTlight
6. SPOTlight uses a seeded topic model to find expression signatures of 
reference cell types and a modified non-negative least squares (NNLS) method 
(denoted NMFreg) to estimate relative cell abundance. The topic model is 
initialized in a way that guides the topics to resemble expression signatures 
of cell types. The default SPOTlight workflow was implemented with 
recommended parameters
(https://github.com/MarcElosua/SPOTlight/tree/

is represented by fewer than 100 cells, all cells are kept), and highly variable genes

and unnormalized expression counts for both snRNA-seq reference and synthetic

SPOTlight package with the following hyper-parameters: transf = ‘uv’ , method = 
‘nsNMF’ , and, crucially, ‘minimum allowed proportions of cell types’ min_cont = 0.01.
NNLS (AutoGeneS)
18,20. NNLS was used to estimate the absolute cell abundance of

each type. Untransformed and unnormalized expression counts of the synthetic 
locations were used as input to NNLS implementation as part of the AutoGeneS 
package. Similarly to cell2location, the output was normalized to produce relative

Mouse brain experiments and analysis. Mouse brain tissue processing. Tissue 
processing. Mouse work was carried out at the Wellcome Sanger Institute in 
accordance with UK Home Office regulations and the UK Animals (Scientific 
Procedures) Act of 1986 under a UK Home Office license, which were regularly 
reviewed by the institutional Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body.
Brains of wild-type adult C57BL/6 mice (postnatal day 56, one female and 
one male) were dissected, snap-frozen, embedded in optimal cutting temperature 
(OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek) and stored at −80 °C. Brain hemispheres were 
cryosectioned at −20 °C using a cryostat (Leica, CM3050S). To assess tissue quality, 
RNA was extracted from test tissue sections using the RNeasy Pico Kit (Qiagen) 
and yielded high RNA integrity number values (9.6 and 9.7) on an Agilent 
Bioanalyzer, indicating high RNA quality.
For matched single-nuclei and Visium RNA-seq experiments, brain 
hemispheres were cryosectioned to adjacent thick (200-µm) and thin (10-µm) 
coronal sections, respectively, and processed the same day. In total, four 
consecutive sets of thick and thin tissue sections were collected from each brain. 
Five sets of tissue sections yielded good-quality single-nuclei data as well as 
good-quality Visium data (three adjacent sections from mouse 1 and two sections 
from mouse 2), and one additional section from mouse 2 yielded good-quality 
single-nuclei data; these were considered for analysis in this study.
snRNA-seq. Thick (200-µm) mouse brain sections were cryosectioned, dissected 
from OCT and kept in a tube on dry ice until subsequent processing. Nuclei 
were extracted from each section as described previously
57. In brief, nuclei were 
released from sections via Dounce homogenization, Hoechst-stained and isolated 
via fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Nuclei were then loaded into the 10x 
Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit (v3) to obtain 3,000–7,000 nuclei per well, and library 
preparation was done per the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on 
an Illumina NovaSeq S4 system.
Visium spatial transcriptomics. Thin (10-µm) mouse brain sections were 
cryosectioned and mounted directly onto separate capture areas on 10x Visium 
Spatial Gene Expression slides (beta product version). Processing was done per 
the manufacturer’s protocols. In brief, sections were methanol-fixed, H&E-stained 
and imaged on a NanoZoomer 2.0 slide scanner (Hamamatsu). Sections were then 
permeabilized and further processed to obtain cDNA libraries that were quality 
controlled using the Agilent Bioanalyzer. The cDNA libraries were sequenced on 
the Illumina HiSeq 4000 system, aiming at 300 million raw reads per section with 
read lengths 28cy R1, 8cy i7 index, 0cy i5 index and 91cy read 2.
Single-nucleus data processing and cell type annotation. Sequencing data were 
processed using 10x Cell Ranger version 3.0.2, aligned to mouse pre-mRNA 
genome reference version mm10 (downloaded from the 10x website), and mRNA 
count matrices were generated by adding intronic and exonic UMI counts for 
each gene in each cell. Initially, snRNA-seq counts were processed using standard 
Seurat V3 workflow without correcting batch effects among six individual samples. 
Specifically, the expression matrices filtered to include droplets containing cells 
(10x Cell Ranger output), were imported into the Seurat object. Cells were filtered 
using the following quality control criteria: number of detected genes per cell 
<5,000, fraction of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes <0.10 and doublet scores 
(scrublet
58) <0.2. The data were then subjected to normalization by a scale factor 
of 10,000 and log1p-transformed (Seurat::NormalizeData), scaled gene-wise by 
subtracting the mean and divided by the standard deviation (Seurat::ScaleData). 
After that, 50 principal components were found (Seurat::RunPCA) and used as 
input to the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithm 
(Seurat::RunUMAP), which was also used for k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph 
construction with k = 100 (Seurat::FindNeighbors). To generate a comprehensive 
cell annotation, joint Louvain clustering with resolution 7 was performed 
(Seurat::FindClusters) on all samples, which yielded 51 cell clusters. Clusters were 
subsequently annotated using marker genes from the literature and existing mouse 
brain single-cell reference datasets
10,16,21. To annotate the regional identity of cell 
types, we used (1) known regional marker genes10,16,21, (2) the spatial expression 
patterns of these genes on in situ hybridization data from the Allen Brain Atlas

smFISH. A wild-type adult C57BL/6 mouse (postnatal day 63, male) was 
transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 
1× PBS. The brain was dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and 
cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4 °C. The brain was then embedded 
in OCT and frozen on isopentane dry ice slurry and stored at −80 °C. Coronal 
cryosections (12-μm) were collected on a cryostat (Leica), mounted onto 
superfrost glass slides (VWR) and stored at −80 °C.
RNAScope smFISH was performed as previously described
60. Tissue slides were 
thawed at room temperature for 15 min and baked at 60 °C for 30 min. Afterwards, 
slides were post-fixed for 15 min in chilled 4% PFA and serially dehydrated 
through 50%, 70%, 100% and 100% ethanol for 5 min each. Four-plex RNAScope 
smFISH was automated using a Leica BOND RX (LS Multiplex Assay, Advanced 
Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne). Tissue sections were permeabilized using heat 
(5 min in Leica BOND ER2 buffer at 95 °C) and protease digestion (Advanced Cell 
Diagnostics Protease III for 20 min). The probes in C1, C2 and C3 channels were 
labeled using Opal 520, 570 and 650 fluorophores (PerkinElmer, diluted 1:1,500), 
respectively. The C4 probe was developed using TSA biotin (TSA Plus Biotin Kit, 
PerkinElmer, 1:500) and streptavidin-conjugated Atto 425 (Sigma-Aldrich, 1:400). 
Nuclei staining was performed with DAPI (Life Technologies, 1:50,000).
Imaging was performed on an Opera Phenix High-Content Screening System 
(PerkinElmer) in spinning disk confocal mode using a ×20 water immersion 
objective (NA 0.16, 298.99 nm per pixel) with a 1-μm z-step size. The excitation 
(Ex) laser and emission (Em) filter wavelengths were as follows: DAPI (375 nm; 
435–480 nm), Atto 425 (425 nm; 463–501 nm), Opal 520 (488 nm; 500–550 nm), 
Opal 570 (561 nm; 570–630 nm) and Opal 650 (640 nm; 650–760 nm). Illumination 
correction and stitching were performed on maximum z-projection images using 
Acapella scripts provided by PerkinElmer.
Astrocyte segmentation and smFISH quantification. The segmentation of astrocytes 
was performed on the Agt image channel with pixel classification of ilastik
61. 
The Agt channel was Gaussian blurred with a sigma of 2 pixels, and the astrocyte 
mask was subsequently generated by the trained pixel classifier. The performance 
of this classifier was evaluated by using visual inspection and a metric called 
out-of-bag (oob) error, which measures prediction error by using bootstrap 
aggregating. The oob error of the classifier was 2.857%, measured on a training 
image of dimension 402 × 2,010 pixels. As Agt is expressed at low levels in 
astrocytes outside the diencephalon
27, we manually traced the diencephalon to 
filter all astrocyte signals outside.
Two rounds of ‘closing’ operations were then executed to connect as many 
major astrocyte cellular processes to the cell bodies as possible. As a consequence, 
some cells were erroneously fused together. To split these fused cells, we performed 
six rounds of opening on large cells (>10,000 pixel
2). Finally, all small partially 
segmented cells (<250 pixel2) were filtered out, and the mean pixel intensities (sum 
intensity/area) of target gene channels were quantified per cell.

transcriptionally distinct, based on the Euclidean distance of their expression 
signatures (linear mRNA count space rather than log-transformed) to the closest 
cell type from the full set of 34 cell types. Euclidean distance represents both the 
number of genes marking each cell type and the absolute expression levels of 
those genes, both of which are important for mapping. Cell types were classified 
as transcriptionally distinct if the Euclidean distance was greater than 100 and as 
transcriptionally fine if it was less than 100 (Supplementary Fig. 22a).

Sample size We aimed to profile 2 mice at 4 adjacent tissue positions (i.e. sections spaced by 200 microns). In total, four consecutive sets of thick and thin 
tissue sections were collected from each brain. Five sets of tissue sections yielded both good quality single nuclei and Visium data (three 
adjacent sections from mouse 1 and two sections from mouse 2) while one additional section from mouse 2 yielded good single nuclei; these 
were sequenced and considered for analysis in this study (Fig 2-3). 
 
For analysis of other tissues and Slide-Seq V2 published spatial transcriptomics datasets were used (human lymph node, n=1; human gut, n=2; 
Slide-Seq V2, n=1) with the total number of samples limited by availability. 
 
No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported previously (references 3, 
25, 42-43). 
Data exclusions Visium spots located outside tissue areas were excluded from all analyses in the paper based on the histological examination. Specifically this 
was done in 2 steps: 
1. Automatic tissue detection using SpaceRanger workflow with subsequence location filtering. No further data exclusion for mouse brain and 
human lymph node data were performed. 
2. When the automatic tissue detection failed (adult human gut samples) - manual annotation was performed, with subsequent filtering of 
location that do not overlap tissue. 
The rationale behind this filtering is as follows: 10X Visium locations are manufactured in a hexagonal grid within square capture areas - as a

Replication For mouse experiments, multiple animals were processed independently on the same day. Fig 2G and Fig S10 report on consistency of 
cell2location cell abundance estimates between adjacent sections and replicate animals. Differences cell type abundances between sections 
can be attributed to change in the regional composition (by histology examination). Cell type locations of novel astrocyte subtypes were 
validated using single molecule FISH of mRNA for gene marking the novel subtypes at corresponding anatomical location in different mice (Fig 
3D) - thus replicating the detection of novel subtypes using alternative technology and an independently processed animal.
